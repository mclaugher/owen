## From the design session, 2026-10-28

- **D31 — Audience: the working engineer, with a stated prerequisite list.** "Working engineer" means any engineer with basic programming proficiency, not only a software engineer. The course states its prerequisites up front. Every lesson keeps one exercise path. The course offers no separate no-code path. A small prerequisite course is a recorded future idea, not a commitment. Why: dual exercise paths double the writing and the maintenance forever; a prerequisite list sets expectations at lower cost.
- **D32 — Practice material embeds in the lesson pages; no practice-repo subdirectory this cycle.** Each post-access lesson packs its practice files inside its own HTML page. A download button saves the practice folder to the learner's machine. Copy-paste blocks are the fallback for a lesson with one or two tiny files. Re-downloading is the reset path. Why: one artifact per lesson, offline-safe, nothing broken sits live in the build repo, and the learner never clones anything. Supersedes the plan's standalone practice repo for lessons A4–A6; later tracks decide for themselves.
- **D33 — A6 ends with "look, not compare."** The final exercise: complete one set task, open the session cost view, and answer three questions about where the tokens went. No published baseline range for Track A. No team-share comparison. Why: the durable habit is reading your own session cost; a published number invites score anxiety and rots when the model lineup changes. Supersedes the Track A baseline-range machinery (D18 still governs any future B/C capstone).
- **D34 — Plain-language rule, enforced.** Every lesson defines an industry term in ordinary words on first use. Genuine industry terms (token, context window) are welcome once defined. Insider words with no industry standing (orchestration, frontier-as-noun, provenance, capstone) are banned from lesson text. A banned-word list lives in the repo. The pre-publish checklist tests lesson text against the list. Why: generation is where jargon creeps in; a checkable list catches it mechanically.
- **D35 — "Suggested experience", not prerequisites.** The list from D31 is framed as encouragement, not a gate: "you will get the most out of this course if you are comfortable with a terminal, know git basics, can read code in one language, and have an editor installed." A learner without these can still take the course. The list sits in a short box on the course index page, with one echo line at the top of lesson 1. Softens D31's "prerequisite" framing; the future-idea note (a small warm-up course) stands.
- **D36 — Track A applied phase: four lessons; lesson 5 is new.** Lesson 4 was overloaded, so it splits instead of cutting content. Lesson 4 keeps the first win plus CLAUDE.md and permission settings. The worked guardrail and the add-on skill move to a new lesson 5, "Guardrails and add-ons." Lesson 5 gets full weight: both halves teach, and both halves practice. The old lessons 5 and 6 slide to 6 and 7. Track A is now seven lessons; every later lesson slides back one number (B1 starts at 0008). Why: five topics overran one 20-minute lesson, and the user chose a split over cuts, naming guardrails an important topic. Supersedes D11's placement ("one worked hook in A4"); D11's spirit stands — one worked example, not a tool tour.
